The 300% Problem

Thirty-two strategies on the BTCUSDT perpetual, against a target of 300% net annual profit at under 20% drawdown. This is the record of what was tested, what survived out of sample, and the measured reason the target is unreachable — which turns out not to be a matter of finding a better signal.

Verdict

No strategy qualified.

Under the 20% drawdown limit the best result is 38.6% net annual — three books run as a portfolio. Lifting that limit does not rescue it: the strongest single book peaks at 59.6% at its growth-optimal size, and past that point more leverage buys less compound return and strictly more drawdown. Section 3 shows the curve.

3 — The binding constraint

Compound growth has a maximum

The intuition that a good strategy can simply be levered to any return target is wrong, and measurably so. Below the growth-optimal size more risk buys more compound return; above it, volatility drag and stop-outs mean more risk buys less return and strictly more drawdown. Each curve below is the same strategy run at twelve position sizes.

S15 convex trend (pyr 3) · peak 59.6% at 5% risk · DD -70%
S15 convex trend (pyr 0) · peak 63.1% at 12% risk · DD -84%
S4 adaptive trend · peak 35.9% at 9% risk · DD -64%

What the curve says

The convex trend book returns +59.6% at 5% risk per trade. At 22% risk — the kind of sizing a 300% target would demand — the same strategy on the same data returns −71.6% with a 100% drawdown. There is no setting in between that reaches 300%, because the peak of the curve is roughly 60% and the peak is a property of the return distribution, not of the parameters.

Under a 20% drawdown budget you are operating at the far left of these curves, around 1–2% risk, which is where the ~23% figures come from. The drawdown limit and the return target are pulling in opposite directions along a curve whose maximum is already an order of magnitude below the target.

Two variants are worth knowing about. Dropping the convex book leaves a two-sleeve pair with a higher Sharpe — 1.43 against 1.34 — but less return at the same drawdown. Adding a fourth sleeve built on open-interest crowding, which correlates just 0.08–0.13 with the other three, gives 37.8% at −19.9% with Sharpe 1.41, profit factor 1.30, 1,090 trades and a shallower worst year (−3% against −8%). On return at the drawdown limit the three are within a point of each other; the four-sleeve version is the better-behaved book, the three-sleeve version the higher-returning one.

The generalisation is the useful part: a sleeve with almost no standalone edge but near-zero correlation is worth more than a strong sleeve correlated 0.7 with what you already hold. The open-interest book alone manages only profit factor 1.13 and a −32% drawdown. That is why the earlier four-sleeve portfolio of similar directional books reached Sharpe 1.12 while two books reading different things reached 1.43.

A second, independent instance of the same caveat cuts the other way. The 6h variant of this book has a higher out-of-sample IC (+0.036 at h=24 against +0.006 for the 12h book chosen here) and yet performs worse out of sample — 17.0% against 18.6%, with a −21.0% drawdown and a bootstrap probability of breaching 20% of 61% against 21%. IC stability neither guarantees nor is required for strategy quality.

One honest caveat. The rank-IC of the traded signal itself degrades out of sample much as the earlier book's did — +0.112 in sample against +0.013 out, at a 96-hour horizon. The strategy nevertheless performs better out of sample, because it trades only the tails beyond |z| > 1 and shapes the payoff with a stop and a target; full-distribution rank-IC does not describe tail behaviour. The stability screen earned its keep by pointing at an under-explored feature family, not by predicting strategy performance directly.

A high-water-mark throttle was built to cap the drawdown of this book — risk tapering to a floor as equity falls below its peak. It works, and it is not worth it: tapering to zero pins drawdown at exactly −20.0% but collapses trade count from 266 to 15 and CAGR to −0.4%. With a 0.15 floor it keeps 263 trades at 30.6% CAGR and −40.9% drawdown. Bounding drawdown by shrinking size costs more return than it saves.

7 — Two things that were not the problem

Ruling out the usual suspects

It was not execution cost

Every result assumed taker execution at 16 bps round-turn. Re-run down a cost ladder to zero:

Free execution is worth about 1.2 percentage points. And the signals killed at the screen stay dead: the 15-minute reversal only crosses profit factor 1.0 at literally zero cost, and the 1h RSI reversion never exceeds 0.96 even free. Shortening the horizon to buy breadth does not work, because the short-horizon signals have no edge to recover at any fee tier.

It was not the lack of a market-neutral sleeve — that was tested too

The earlier version of this study leaned on a long-spot / short-perp carry, which is out of scope here. Its futures-native replacement — perpetual versus quarterly delivery futures, 24 contracts and 75,823 hourly bars — was measured directly: the quarterly carry runs +8.6% annualised and perp funding +9.9%, so the tradable spread between them averages −1.3% and is positive 49% of the time. Once both legs are futures the funding sits on both sides and the spread prices to zero, exactly as arbitrage should. There is no futures-native carry to harvest.

The breakout pair is itself a result

Costs subtract from both directions, so inverting a post-cost PF of 0.83 does not hand you 1.20. But if range breaks were pure noise, the two sides would lose roughly symmetrically around the cost drag. The fade losing materially more than the breakout (0.60–0.76 against 0.83–0.97) says breakouts carry weak genuine continuation — real, and far too small to clear 16 bps. Together with the volatility-squeeze version (PF 0.85–0.98) that closes the whole intraday-breakout family.

The tick result closes the data question

The correlation between the tick-computed order imbalance and the one already available in the klines is 0.9999. The genuinely new features — who is trading, whether they are splitting orders, what their flow costs in impact — carry residual IC of 0.003 to 0.015 against forward returns at horizons from 5 minutes to 4 hours, which is noise at n = 43,200. The 51 GB full-history download is not justified. Every source Binance publishes for this instrument has now been mined: OHLCV, taker volume, trade count, funding, open interest, trader positioning, quarterly futures, the coin-margined contract, and tick prints. Only the order book remains, and that is a market-making dataset rather than a directional-signal one.

Two things that did check out

Execution granularity. Every result resolved stops on 15-minute bars with the stop assumed to fill first when a bar straddled both levels. Re-run on the full 1-minute history (3,506,400 bars, zero gaps): S7 goes 22.9% → 21.8%, and the convex book's drawdown improves from −36.0% to −30.5% because the worst-case rule fires less often on a finely resolved path. The 15-minute grid was mildly optimistic on return and pessimistic on drawdown. No conclusion changes.

Parameter ensembling. Running all 72 configurations of the S7 book simultaneously at 1/N size, rather than selecting one, lifts Sharpe from a median single-config 1.34 to 1.54 in sample and 0.73 to 0.80 out of sample. Single configurations ranged from −0.6% to +36.0% CAGR out of sample; that spread is precisely the selection risk the grid search exposed. This is the right default. It does not move the ceiling.

Averaging dilutes, and gating cannot rescue

Equal-weight averaging dragged the strongest signal toward the weakest twice — S2 took profit factor from 1.30 to 1.19, and blending breadth into the positioning composite took IC from 0.108 to 0.070. Conditional gating is the usual answer, and it worked in S1 (profit factor 1.04 to 1.30). Here it did not: every gate that actually removed trades hurt, and the one variant matching the baseline vetoed just 12 of 229 trades — it converges to doing nothing. Gating works when the confirmer is genuinely predictive. Breadth was not, and no combination rule rescues a signal with no out-of-sample edge.

The sharpest methodological lesson

The breadth signal's in-sample decile economics were the most convincing in the whole study outside the winner: top decile +131.9 bps over 96 hours, t = 10.9, +115.9 bps net of cost, and only 0.22 correlated with the incumbent. Traded, it lost money in every out-of-sample variant. A t-statistic of 10.9 computed on overlapping 96-hour windows is far weaker evidence than it appears, and in-sample decile economics are not evidence of out-of-sample tradability.

7c — The selection rule was the problem

What a stability screen does to 91 features

Every screen up to this point ranked features by in-sample rank-IC and built on the winner. The breadth signal exposed that as unsafe: top decile +131.9 bps over 96 hours at t = 10.9, and it lost money in every out-of-sample variant. So the screen was rebuilt — measure the same IC separately in each window, and keep only what holds its sign and most of its strength in both.

Applied to all 91 features on 4h bars, at 24-hour and 96-hour horizons:

  • 41 of 91 (45%) keep their sign in both windows at both horizons — barely better than a coin flip.
  • Of the 33 with |IS IC| > 0.04, only 18 are sign-stable, and the median out-of-sample retention is 0.27: a strong-looking feature keeps about a quarter of its apparent power.

An audit of this report's own headline

The screen flags tt_vs_retail — the feature S7 is named after — as the single biggest mirage in the panel. Yet S7 reported in-sample 22.3% against out-of-sample 24.1%. Both cannot be innocent, so I checked directly, and the answer is in three parts.

The composite the strategy actually trades degrades but does not collapse — on 4h bars, IC at h=96 goes +0.082 → +0.014, and at h=168 +0.072 → +0.034. But the component carrying it out of sample is not the one in the name. At h=96: tt_vs_retail +0.104 → −0.003, while −retail_acct_z holds +0.083 → +0.017 and −tt_acct_z holds +0.126 → +0.023. What survives is fading a crowded retail book and a crowded count of top-trader accounts — not following sophisticated positioning.

It is not disguised long-bias beta, the other suspicion. Net exposure is −0.07 in sample and +0.02 out of sample, and the short book is profitable in both windows — including out of sample, where the perpetual rose 25%. Shorts earning into a rising market is alpha, not beta. Longs do supply 85–91% of gross P&L, so the book is long-driven, but it is not long-only in disguise.

Net effect on this report's conclusion: none — S7 still clears four of five gates. But the honest characterisation is weaker than the earlier framing: the edge loses roughly three-quarters of its strength out of sample, and it rests on crowd-fading rather than on smart-money following.

8 — Why the target is out of reach

The arithmetic behind the shortfall

The measured ceiling

Section 3 is the answer: compound growth peaks near 60% CAGR and declines beyond it. That peak is not a parameter — it follows from the return distribution, and every strategy in this study lands in the same place. Under a 20% drawdown budget you operate far below the peak, at roughly 23%.

Where the ceiling comes from

Sharpe ≈ IC × √(independent bets per year)

The strongest predictor found was top-trader-versus-retail positioning at rank-IC 0.108 on a 96-hour horizon. One instrument at that horizon gives about 8,766 ÷ 96 ≈ 91 non-overlapping bets a year, so 0.108 × √91 ≈ 1.03 — almost exactly the Sharpe of 1.14 the strategy actually delivered. The model is not being pessimistic; it is describing what happened.

Every route to more breadth on one instrument was tested and closed. Shorter horizons: IC collapses to 0.013 at 1h, and the 1h book's apparent Sharpe advantage was in-sample only (1.48 → 0.72 out of sample). More sleeves: the four perp books correlate 0.25–0.70 because they are all directional on the same asset, so Sharpe stays at 1.10. Cheaper fills: worth 1.2 percentage points. Convexity: raises profit factor to 1.91, leaves Sharpe at 0.95.

And the grid search proves the danger of pushing harder

S16 searched 480 configurations under an explicit drawdown budget and selected on in-sample data. Under a 40% budget it found an in-sample CAGR of 73.3%. Out of sample that configuration returned 20.2% with a 54% drawdown. Under a 20% budget: in-sample 31.1%, out-of-sample 11.0%, and the drawdown breached to 23%. This is what "trying harder" produces once the real edge is exhausted — larger in-sample numbers and no out-of-sample improvement.

What 300% at 20% drawdown would actually require

A Calmar ratio of 15. The best measured in this study is 1.23, and across every strategy the ratio sits between 0.8 and 1.4. Reaching 15 needs a Sharpe near 11, or roughly 10,400 independent bets a year at the IC available. One instrument at a horizon where the signal works provides 91. That gap is filled either by a wide cross-section of instruments — outside a BTCUSDT-only brief — or by sub-second market making, where the edge is spread capture and the bet count runs to millions, and which cannot be honestly evaluated from OHLCV data because its return is a function of latency and fee tier rather than of any signal.

Cost stressFinalists re-run at 1.5× and 2× costs, and down to zero (section 7)

Look-ahead controls

  • Indicators for bar t use only bars ≤ t. A signal formed at the close of a 4h bar executes at the open of the next 15-minute bar, never earlier.
  • Stops and targets resolve on 15-minute bars. Where one bar spans both levels, the stop fills first.
  • The order-flow orthogonalisation refits monthly on an expanding window of strictly past data. A full-sample regression would have leaked, and did in the first draft.
  • Machine-learning walk-forwards use a purge gap equal to the label horizon.
  • Portfolio weights are estimated in-sample and held fixed out-of-sample.
  • Funding and positioning observations are keyed to when they became public, not to the bar they describe.
